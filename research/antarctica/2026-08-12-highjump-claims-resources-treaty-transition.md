# Antarctica — Highjump, Claims, Resources, and Treaty Transition

Status: JPV Labs verification note
Date: 2026-08-12

## Highjump was not merely scientific

Operation Highjump (U.S. Naval Antarctic Developments Program, 1946–47) was a major Navy expedition immediately after World War II. National Archives and Naval History records describe its documented purposes as establishing Little America IV, training personnel, testing ships/equipment in polar conditions, assessing the feasibility of bases, exploration, charting, and aerial photography. Navy history also states the expedition sought to explore and chart largely unknown areas and determine the feasibility of military stations and operations in the polar region.

Primary sources:
- National Archives: https://unwritten-record.blogs.archives.gov/2017/09/27/operation-hi-jump-exploring-antarctica-with-the-u-s-navy/
- Naval History and Heritage Command, Burton Island: https://www.history.navy.mil/content/history/nhhc/research/histories/ship-histories/danfs/b/burton-island-i.html
- Naval History and Heritage Command, Jan. 29 chronology: https://www.history.navy.mil/today-in-history/january-29.html

## The expedition immediately triggered sovereignty questions

A confidential State Department memorandum dated 15 Nov. 1946 explicitly says publicity around the Navy-Byrd expedition raised the issue of conflicting international claims and framed the core question as who owned what. The U.S. position at that time was to recognize no foreign claims, assert none officially, but reserve its rights to make claims later.

A 22 Nov. 1946 State Department telegram separately describes the Navy expedition as a task-force exercise concerned with techniques for operations in the Antarctic climate, while distinguishing it from Finn Ronne's private scientific expedition.

Primary sources:
- FRUS 1946, d786: https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1946v01/d786
- FRUS 1946, d788: https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1946v01/d788

## U.S. personnel were instructed how to deposit claims during Highjump

A December 1946 State Department document provides a model declaration for members of the 1947 Naval Antarctic Developments Project to deposit records claiming discovered/investigated territory in the name of the United States. The guidance required copies to be retained and transmitted to Washington, while recommending no public announcement without specific authorization.

Primary source:
- FRUS 1946, d790: https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1946v01/d790

Finding: Highjump had a documented latent territorial/legal function alongside military logistics, exploration, mapping, and science. This is evidence of human sovereignty strategy, not evidence of a hidden non-human actor.

## Highjump created mapping data that needed follow-on ground control

Operation Windmill (1947–48) followed Highjump and specifically obtained ground-control points needed to accurately map geographic features seen in Highjump aerial photographs. It also conducted hydrographic and geological surveys, ice reconnaissance, and other field work.

Primary source:
- Naval History and Heritage Command, Burton Island: https://www.history.navy.mil/content/history/nhhc/research/histories/ship-histories/danfs/b/burton-island-i.html

Finding: the 1946–48 sequence is technically coherent: broad aerial reconnaissance first, then geodetic ground control to convert imagery into accurate maps.

## Before the Treaty, U.S. policy explicitly included natural-resource access

U.S. national-security policy documents from the mid-1950s list objectives that included freedom of Antarctic exploration and mapping, improvement of the U.S. position regarding sovereignty, and access by the United States and friendly powers to Antarctic natural resources. Other documents discuss the possibility of hidden strategic materials and the need for continued mapping/geological work because Antarctic military/economic value was not yet known.

Primary sources:
- FRUS 1955–57, d333: https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1955-57v11/d333
- FRUS 1955–57, d330: https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1955-57v11/d330
- FRUS 1955–57, d312: https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1955-57v11/d312

Finding: resource access and strategic-material uncertainty were real policy concerns before 1959. The later Antarctic Treaty did not initially settle mineral-resource exploitation; that became a major treaty-system question later.

## The United States seriously considered making an Antarctic territorial claim

Policy papers repeatedly identify the 90°W–150°W sector as an unclaimed area where U.S. exploration gave the United States a strong basis for a potential claim. A draft presidential proclamation in 1957 even described a potential U.S. sovereignty claim from 90°W to 150°W, extending poleward. Other documents state the U.S. lacked enough knowledge to make an informed choice about the best claim locations and wanted continued mapping and exploration first.

Primary sources:
- FRUS 1955–57, d311: https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1955-57v11/d311
- FRUS 1955–57, d338: https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1955-57v11/d338
- FRUS 1955–57, d299: https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1955-57v11/d299

Finding: one reason extensive mapping mattered was sovereignty. Exploration, occupancy, discovery, mapping, and stations were treated as evidence relevant to territorial claims.

## Soviet activity was a direct driver of the policy transition

U.S. records from 1957–58 repeatedly express concern about Soviet stations, aerial photography, mapping, hydrographic charting, geology, and the possibility that sustained Soviet presence might support future Soviet claims. U.S. planners explicitly recognized that a U.S. territorial claim might jeopardize IGY scientific cooperation and could provoke a Soviet claim.

Primary sources:
- FRUS 1958–60, d269: https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1958-60v02/d269
- FRUS 1955–57, d330: https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1955-57v11/d330

## The 1958–59 pivot was from competition over sovereignty toward a governed freeze

By 1958 U.S. policy had shifted toward a treaty architecture built around peaceful use, scientific freedom, inspection, and freezing the existing legal status of claims. A Sept. 1959 U.S. position paper says an acceptable treaty had to include peaceful use, effective inspection rights, freezing the claims status quo, and scientific freedom.

Primary source:
- FRUS 1958–60, d291: https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1958-60v02/d291

This is the core institutional transition:

`expedition + mapping + latent claims + resource/strategic uncertainty + Soviet competition -> fear of claim escalation -> preservation of IGY cooperation -> freeze sovereignty -> establish inspection/science regime`.

## Why 60°S?

Conference records show 60°S was consciously negotiated as the Treaty area's geographic boundary. Argentina proposed including lands, waters, and airspace south of 60°S; Japan and France raised questions about sea ice/permanent ice; the UK proposed the area south of 60°S including islands and ice shelves while excluding the high seas. The final Article VI uses the geographic parallel rather than a moving ice boundary.

Primary sources:
- FRUS 1958–60, d299: https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1958-60v02/d299
- FRUS 1958–60, d302: https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1958-60v02/d302
- FRUS 1958–60, d303: https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1958-60v02/d303

Finding: 60°S was chosen because negotiators needed a stable legal line that could govern land, islands, ice shelves, and associated activity without defining the regime by a moving natural ice edge.

## Resource knowledge at Treaty time was incomplete

A 1958 U.S. policy paper describes Antarctica as having no present economic value while explicitly noting possible future values that could become important with improved knowledge and technology. Later USGS reviews say most geological/resource information was accumulated since the 1957–58 IGY, and that by 1983 there were still no known petroleum or mineral resources, though geological evidence made various deposits plausible.

Primary sources:
- FRUS 1958–60, d269: https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1958-60v02/d269
- USGS, Petroleum and mineral resources of Antarctica: https://www.usgs.gov/publications/petroleum-and-mineral-resources-antarctica
- USGS, Mineral resources of Antarctica: https://www.usgs.gov/publications/mineral-resources-antarctica

Finding: the 1959 Treaty was not a settlement built around a proven treasure map. It was a governance solution created while geological/resource uncertainty remained high.

## Later resource politics confirm that mineral access was a real governance problem

By the 1970s U.S. policy documents were explicitly addressing Antarctic mineral exploration and the possibility that commercial-resource activity could destabilize the Treaty claims compromise. Treaty parties later negotiated a mineral-resource convention in the 1980s, but the 1991 Environmental Protocol instead established the current prohibition on mineral-resource activity other than scientific research.

Primary sources:
- FRUS 1969–76, d59: https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76ve03/d59
- USGS 1990 resource-policy summary: https://www.usgs.gov/publications/recent-geophysical-and-geological-research-antarctica-related-assessment-petroleum
- Antarctic Treaty Secretariat Protocol: https://www.ats.aq/e/protocol.html

## Current interpretation

The strongest verified explanation for the compressed social-media story is not `they found a wall and hid what was behind it`.

It is:

`Antarctica was poorly mapped -> postwar powers rapidly expanded aerial/geological knowledge -> mapping strengthened territorial positions -> governments worried about strategic resources and Soviet presence -> competing claims risked conflict -> IGY demonstrated cooperation -> Treaty froze sovereignty and institutionalized inspection/science -> later resource pressure was handled through additional governance`.

This chain is entirely human, documentary, and visible in primary records.

2. The claim-record locations deposited/dropped by U.S. personnel during Highjump and whether all surviving copies are publicly catalogued.
3. The maps attached to 1950s State/NSC policy papers showing proposed U.S. claims and rights.
4. Soviet Antarctic mapping and geology results from the 1956–58 period and how they compared with U.S. knowledge.
5. Geological/resource intelligence available to treaty negotiators but not summarized in the diplomatic memoranda.
6. How the permanent-ice versus fixed-latitude debate affected the final Article VI geometry.
7. Whether modern Bedmap/BedMachine terrain materially overlaps areas the U.S. in the 1950s considered most strategically or economically valuable.
